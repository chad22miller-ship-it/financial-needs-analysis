How money you take out could be taxed

This hypothetical illustration shows how taking money out of a 401(k) or IRA, a taxable account and a Roth account could be taxed in 2026, using the federal figures the IRS published. Change the amount, your other income and your filing status to see how the picture might move. It is a teaching tool, not tax advice.

Three places money can come out of

Tax-deferred

Like a traditional 401(k) or IRA

The whole withdrawal counts as ordinary income, taxed in steps called brackets, the same way pay is.

Taxable

A regular brokerage account

Only the profit is taxed, not the money you put in. Held more than a year, profit is taxed at 0%, 15% or 20%. Held a year or less, it is taxed like pay.

Tax-exempt

Like a Roth 401(k) or Roth IRA

Money goes in after tax. A qualified withdrawal may come out with no federal income tax.

In plain English

How you get taxed

Ordinary income is taxed in steps

The tax system uses brackets. Only the part of your income that falls inside a bracket is taxed at that bracket's rate, so moving into a higher bracket does not re-tax the dollars below it. A withdrawal from a 401(k) or traditional IRA counts as ordinary income, like pay. The standard deduction ( for this filing status in 2026) comes off first.

Long-term gains: 0%, 15% or 20%

Profit on something held more than a year is a long-term gain. It is stacked on top of your other taxable income, and where it lands sets the rate: 0% up to of taxable income, 15% up to , and 20% above that, for this filing status in 2026.

Short-term gains are taxed like pay

Profit on something held a year or less is a short-term gain. It is added to your ordinary income and taxed in the same brackets as pay, so it could cost more than the same profit held longer.

An extra 3.8% at higher incomes

The net investment income tax adds 3.8% on investment income, such as gains, when total income is over for this filing status. It applies to the smaller of the investment income or the amount over that line. The IRS does not count 401(k) or IRA withdrawals as investment income for it.

Taking money out early, and withholding

Before age 59½, a 401(k) or IRA withdrawal can carry an extra 10% tax on the taxable part, unless an exception applies. Separately, a 401(k) payout paid to you usually has 20% withheld, and an IRA payout 10% unless you choose otherwise. Withholding is a prepayment toward your tax, not the final amount.

A Roth works differently

Roth money goes in after tax. A qualified withdrawal is not taxed: the account has been open at least five tax years, and you are 59½ or older, disabled, or the money goes to a beneficiary after death (or up to $10,000 toward a first home from a Roth IRA). Taking out earnings early can be taxed and may carry the 10% tax.

How this is calculated
  • Each account's figure is the federal tax with the withdrawal minus the federal tax without it, on the other income you entered, so the standard deduction and the brackets are used the way a tax return uses them.
  • Tax-deferred: the whole amount is ordinary income. If you choose "Under 59½", an extra 10% of the amount is added for the early-withdrawal tax. Exceptions are not modeled.
  • Taxable: only the share you mark as profit counts. Held more than a year, that profit is stacked on top of your other taxable income and taxed at 0%, 15% or 20%. Held a year or less, it is taxed like pay. Either way, the 3.8% tax can apply when income is over the line shown above.
  • Tax-exempt: shown as $0 on the assumption that the withdrawal is a qualified one.
  • A state tax, if you choose a rate, is that flat percent of the income counted (the whole amount for a 401(k) or IRA, the profit for a taxable account). Real state rules differ.
  • "Tax on the next dollar" is the extra tax on the next $100 taken out, as a percent. It is approximate and can change at a bracket line.
  • An RMD is the balance on the prior December 31 divided by the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table number for the age you reach that year, taxed as ordinary income on top of your other income.
